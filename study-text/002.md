## Slide 2

1. INTRODUCTION
Arteriosclerosis is characterized by thickening, hardening, and loss of elasticity of arterial walls.
It makes arteries stiff and can interfere with normal blood flow to organs and tissues.
Develops gradually and is more common with increasing age.
Risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, obesity, and physical inactivity can accelerate the process.
Complications may include coronary artery disease, heart attack, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, and kidney problems.

## Slide 3

2. DEFINITION
Arteriosclerosis is the thickening and hardening of the walls of arteries.
It causes arteries to become less elastic and makes it more difficult for blood to flow normally.

## Slide 4

3. AETIOLOGY
Ageing – arteries naturally become less elastic with age.
Hypertension – prolonged high blood pressure damages arterial walls.
Diabetes mellitus – high blood glucose contributes to vascular damage.
Dyslipidemia/high LDL cholesterol – promotes fatty plaque formation.
Smoking – damages the arterial lining and promotes inflammation.
Obesity – increases cardiovascular risk.
Physical inactivity – contributes to cardiovascular risk.
Family history/genetic factors – may increase susceptibility.
Chronic inflammation and certain diseases – may contribute to arterial damage.

## Slide 5

4. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
Normally, arteries are flexible and elastic, allowing them to expand and recoil as blood flows through them.
Ageing, hypertension, diabetes, and other factors cause changes in the arterial wall.
The wall becomes thicker, harder, and less elastic due to increased fibrous tissue and, in some forms, calcium deposition.
Stiff arteries cannot expand and recoil normally, interfering with blood flow and increasing vascular resistance.
Reduced blood flow decreases oxygen and nutrient delivery to tissues.
Prolonged reduction in blood supply can cause ischemia and eventually tissue or organ damage.

## Slide 6

5. TYPES
Atherosclerosis – buildup of fatty substances and cholesterol-containing plaques inside arteries, causing narrowing and reduced blood flow.
Arteriolosclerosis – thickening and narrowing of small arteries (arterioles), commonly associated with hypertension and diabetes mellitus.
Mönckeberg Medial Calcific Sclerosis – calcium deposits develop in the middle layer of muscular arteries, causing stiffness.

## Slide 7

6. SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS
May have no symptoms in early stages; symptoms depend on the arteries affected.
Chest pain or angina when coronary arteries are affected.
Shortness of breath.
Fatigue or weakness.
Pain in the legs during walking (intermittent claudication).
Cold or numb hands and feet.
Weak or absent peripheral pulses.
Dizziness or confusion when blood flow to the brain is reduced.
Vision or speech problems and sudden weakness may occur when it contributes to a stroke.

## Slide 8

7. INVESTIGATIONS
History Taking – symptoms, hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, heart disease, smoking, diet, physical activity, and family history.
Physical Examination – blood pressure, peripheral pulses, skin colour and temperature, signs of poor circulation, and arterial bruits.
Blood Tests – lipid profile; blood glucose / HbA1c.
Doppler Ultrasound – assesses blood flow and identifies narrowing or blockage.
ECG – records electrical activity of the heart and may show associated heart problems.
CT Angiography (CTA) – detailed images of blood vessels to identify narrowing or blockage.
Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) – uses MRI to visualize arteries and assess blood flow.
Angiography – contrast dye and X-rays identify significant narrowing or blockage.

## Slide 9

8. MEDICAL MANAGEMENT
1. Lifestyle Modification: healthy balanced diet low in saturated fats and salt; regular physical activity; healthy weight; avoid smoking.
2. Medications:
Cholesterol-lowering: Atorvastatin, Simvastatin, Ezetimibe.
Antiplatelet: Aspirin, Clopidogrel.
Antihypertensive: Amlodipine, Lisinopril.
Antidiabetic: Metformin, Insulin.
3. Surgical / Procedural Treatment: Angioplasty, stent placement, and bypass surgery may be required in severe narrowing or blockage.

## Slide 10

8. NURSING MANAGEMENT
Monitor the patient: vital signs, pain, peripheral pulses, skin colour, and skin temperature.
Administer prescribed medications and monitor effects and possible side effects.
Promote healthy habits: healthy diet, appropriate physical activity, weight management, and smoking avoidance.
Patient education: explain the disease, medications, treatment adherence, and regular follow-up.
Monitor for complications: observe for reduced circulation and cardiovascular complications; report abnormalities promptly.
Provide psychological support: reassure the patient and encourage cooperation with treatment.

## Slide 11

9. NURSING DIAGNOSES
Ineffective Peripheral Tissue Perfusion related to reduced arterial blood flow secondary to arterial narrowing, as evidenced by cold extremities, weak peripheral pulses, pallor, numbness, or pain.
Acute Pain related to inadequate tissue oxygenation secondary to reduced arterial circulation, as evidenced by pain, especially during activity.
Activity Intolerance related to an imbalance between oxygen supply and demand, as evidenced by fatigue, weakness, or pain during physical activity.
Deficient Knowledge related to inadequate information about the disease, medications, diet, exercise, and lifestyle modifications.
Risk for Impaired Skin Integrity related to decreased peripheral circulation and reduced tissue oxygenation.

Kidney problems
Tissue ischemia and damage

## Slide 13

11. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES
Vasculitis – inflammation of blood vessels that may cause narrowing or blockage.
Thromboembolism – a blood clot that blocks an artery and can cause reduced blood flow.
Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) – reduced blood flow to the limbs, commonly caused by atherosclerotic narrowing.
Fibromuscular dysplasia – abnormal growth of arterial wall tissue that can cause arterial narrowing, especially in renal or carotid arteries.
Aortic aneurysm – weakening and enlargement of an arterial wall.
