## Slide 2

INTRODUCTION / OVERVIEW
• The Glasgow Coma Scale is a standardized neurological assessment tool used to assess a patient’s level of consciousness.
• It was developed in 1974 by Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett.
• The GCS evaluates a patient’s response through three components:
=> Eye Opening (E)
=> Verbal Response (V)
=> Motor Response (M)
• The scores from the three components are combined to produce a total score ranging from 3 to 15.
• It is commonly used in patients with head injuries, altered consciousness, neurological conditions and critically ill patients.
• Repeated GCS assessments help healthcare professionals identify changes in neurological status.
Note: The GCS does not diagnose the cause of unconsciousness. Instead, it provides a standardized way of describing and monitoring the patient’s level of consciousness.

## Slide 3

DEFINITION
What is the Glasgow Coma Scale?
The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a clinical scoring system used to objectively assess the level of consciousness and responsiveness of a patient.
It assesses three areas:
• E = Eye Opening: 1–4
• V = Verbal Response: 1–5
• M = Motor Response: 1–6
Formula: GCS = E + V + M
Score range:
Minimum = 3
Maximum = 15
A higher score generally indicates a higher level of consciousness, while a lower score indicates a greater impairment of consciousness.

## Slide 4

PURPOSE OF GLASGOW COMA SCALE
The GCS is used to:
Assess the patient’s initial level of consciousness.
Establish a neurological baseline.
Monitor changes in the patient’s neurological condition.
Identify neurological deterioration.
Monitor recovery or improvement.
Provide a standardized method of communicating a patient’s neurological status.
Assist healthcare professionals in determining the severity of impaired consciousness.
Help monitor patients following trauma or head injury.
Important: Serial/ repeated GCS assessments are important because a change in score may indicate deterioration or improvement.

## Slide 5

TYPES OF STIMULUS USED IN GCS
Verbal stimulus
The patient is spoken to using:
• A normal voice initially.
• Clear questions.
• Simple commands.
Examples:
“Can you open your eyes?”
“What is your name?”
“Can you lift your arm?”
The patient’s response is observed and scored.
2. Physical pressure stimulus
If the patient does not respond adequately to voice, an appropriate physical pressure stimulus may be used according to clinical protocol.
The purpose is to determine whether the patient can produce a response to stimulation.
Physical stimulation can include:
• Central stimulation — used to assess an overall response, such as appropriate pressure to a central area according to clinical protocol.
• Peripheral stimulation — applied to a limb, such as pressure to the fingertip/nail bed when appropriate.
Note: The term “painful stimulus” is commonly used in older teaching, but “pressure stimulus” is preferred in modern GCS terminology.

## Slide 7

SCORING SYSTEM
Eye Opening = E
• E4 = Spontaneous: Eyes are open without stimulation.
• E3 = To voice: Eyes open when the patient is spoken to.
• E2 = To pressure: Eyes open only after physical stimulation.
• E1 = None: No eye opening despite appropriate stimulation.
5
Verbal Response = V
• V5 = Oriented: Patient communicates appropriately and is oriented.
• V4 = Confused: Patient can talk but is disoriented or confused.
• V3 = Inappropriate words: Recognizable words are spoken, but they are not appropriate or meaningful conversation.
• V2 = Incomprehensible sounds: Moaning, groaning or other sounds without recognizable words.
• V1 = None: No verbal response.
Motor Response = M
• M6 = Obeys commands: Performs a simple command appropriately.
• M5 = Localizes to pressure: Makes a purposeful movement toward the stimulus.
• M4 = Withdraws from pressure: Pulls the limb away from the stimulus.
• M3 = Abnormal flexion: Abnormal flexor posturing (decorticate pattern).
• M2 = Extension: Abnormal extensor posturing (decerebrate pattern).
• M1 = None: No motor response.

## Slide 8

Interpretation
🟢 13–15: Generally categorized as mild impairment.
🟡 9–12: Generally categorized as moderate impairment.
🔴 ≤8: Generally categorized as severe impairment and warrants urgent clinical assessment.
These categories are general guides, not stand alone diagnoses or management rules.
Always consider the individual components, clinical context and the patient's trend over time.

## Slide 9

Nursing Responsibilities
• Establish and record a baseline GCS.
• Perform assessments consistently and compare with previous findings.
• Record E, V and M separately.
• Monitor vital signs, pupils and other neurological findings.
• Recognize and promptly report deterioration in consciousness.
• Maintain patient safety.
• Document factors that could affect the assessment.

## Slide 10

LIMITATIONS OF GCS
Although GCS is very useful, it has limitations.
Factors that may affect the score:
Sedative or analgesic medications
Alcohol or drug intoxication
Language barriers
Hearing impairment
Speech impairment
Aphasia
Pre-existing neurological or cognitive conditions
Eye or facial injuries
Endotracheal intubation
Young age/developmental limitations
Other limitations:
GCS does not provide a complete neurological examination.
It does not assess:
Pupillary response
Cranial nerves
Sensation
Reflexes
Vital signs
• Different factors can make one or more components difficult or impossible to assess.

SUMMARY
Key Points
• The Glasgow Coma Scale assesses a patient’s level of consciousness.
• It has three components:
Eye — E
Verbal — V
Motor — M
The total score ranges from 3 to 15.
GCS = E + V + M
Maximum score = 15
Minimum score = 3
GCS should be assessed systematically and documented clearly.
Serial assessments are important for detecting changes.
Always document the individual component scores.
A low or decreasing GCS requires prompt clinical attention.
GCS has limitations and should be combined with a complete neurological assessment.
