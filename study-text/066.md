MALARIA is a life-threatening disease caused by parasites of the Plasmodium genus, transmitted to humans by the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It is both preventable and curable, but if left untreated, it can progress to severe illness and death. The two most dangerous species are  P. falciparum  and  P. vivax . 

Slide 3
OVERVIEW Malaria remains one of the most significant public health problems worldwide Globally in 2023, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597 000 malaria deaths in 83 countries The WHO African Region carries a disproportionately high share of the global malaria burden In 2023, the WHO African Region was home to 94% of malaria cases (246 million) and 95% (569 000) of malaria deaths Children under 5 accounted for about 76% of all malaria deaths in the Region.

Slide 4
Over half of these deaths occurred in four countries: Nigeria (30.9%), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (11.3%), Niger (5.9%) and United Republic of Tanzania (4.3%) Getting treatment early for mild malaria can stop the infection from becoming severe.  Malaria infection during pregnancy can also cause premature delivery or delivery of a baby with low birth weight.

Cause  Malaria is caused by infection with  protozoan parasites of the genus  Plasmodium Parasite 

Slide 6
TYPES OF PLASMODIUM Plasmodium falciparum  – the most dangerous and common in Africa, responsible for severe malaria and most deaths. Plasmodium vivax  – more common in Asia and Latin America, can cause relapses due to dormant liver forms (hypnozoites). Plasmodium  malariae  – less common, usually causes milder but chronic infections. Plasmodium  ovale  – rare, found mainly in West Africa, also capable of relapses. Plasmodium  knowlesi  – a zoonotic species (from monkeys), found mainly in Southeast Asia.

Slide 7
MODE OF TRANSMISSION  Mosquito bite : Bite of an infected female  Anopheles  mosquito carrying  Plasmodium  parasites. Other (less common) Causes Blood transfusion  from an infected donor. Organ transplantation  from an infected donor. Congenital transmission  from mother to child during pregnancy or delivery. Needle-sharing  or accidental exposure to infected blood 

Slide 8
RISK FACTORS  1. Living in malaria-endemic areas (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia) 2. Proximity to mosquito breeding sites (stagnant water, swamps) 3. Rainy season or high rainfall periods 4. Children under five years of age 5. Pregnant women, especially first and second pregnancies 6. Weakened immune system (HIV/AIDS, malnutrition) 7. Poor housing conditions (no window/door screens) 8. Lack of preventive measures (insecticide-treated nets, indoor spraying) 9. Delayed access to healthcare or treatment 10. Travelers or migrants to endemic areas 11. Genetic factors (e.g., lack of protective traits like sickle cell trait)

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY Malaria is caused by  Plasmodium  parasites, most commonly  Plasmodium falciparum  in Africa, which infect humans through the bite of an infected female  Anopheles  mosquito  When the mosquito bites, it injects sporozoites into the bloodstream, which travel to the liver and invade hepatocytes  Within liver cells, the parasites multiply asymptomatically, producing merozoites over several days.  In the case of  P. vivax  and  P.  ovale , some parasites can form dormant liver stages called hypnozoites, which may cause relapses weeks or months later 

Slide 12
Once released from the liver, merozoites invade red blood cells (RBCs), where they continue to multiply through the trophozoite and schizont stages.  Infected RBCs eventually rupture, releasing more merozoites into the bloodstream, which leads to the characteristic cyclical fever, chills, and anemia associated with malaria.  In  P. falciparum  infections, infected RBCs express adhesion molecules that allow them to stick to blood vessel walls, leading to sequestration in vital organs such as the brain, kidneys, and lungs, and resulting in severe complications like cerebral malaria, renal failure, or pulmonary edema.

SIGNS AND SYMPTOMS  General symptoms Fever (cyclical, every 48–72 hours) Chills and rigors Sweating Headache Fatigue and malaise Muscle and joint pains Severe Malaria Features (mainly  P. falciparum ):  Cerebral malaria (impaired consciousness, seizures, coma) Respiratory distress or pulmonary edema Renal failure or dark urine  Hypoglycemia  Severe anemia Shock Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Nausea and vomiting Abdominal pain Diarrhea (less common) Hematologic Signs: Anemia Pallor Jaundice Other Signs in Children:  Lethargy and poor feeding Recurrent high fevers Splenomegaly

Slide 17
DIAGNOSIS / INVESTIGATIONS History and Physical Examination Fever pattern : intermittent, cyclical (every 48–72 hours depending on species) Recent travel  to or residence in malaria-endemic areas Symptoms : chills, rigors, sweating, headache, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, lethargy Past malaria episodes  or previous treatment Vaccination and prophylaxis history  (e.g., use of insecticide-treated nets, chemoprophylaxis in travelers)

Slide 18
PHYSICAL EXAMINATION General appearance: pallor, jaundice, lethargy Vital signs: fever, tachycardia, hypotension (in severe cases) Signs of dehydration Abdominal exam: hepatomegaly, splenomegaly Neurological assessment: altered consciousness, seizures (for cerebral malaria) Respiratory assessment: signs of respiratory distress or pulmonary edema

Slide 19
LABORATORY TESTS Microscopy (Gold standard) : Thick and thin blood smears to detect and quantify  Plasmodium  parasites Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs) : Detect  Plasmodium  antigens; useful where microscopy is unavailable Complete Blood Count (CBC) : Check for anemia, thrombocytopenia Biochemistry  (if severe malaria suspected): Blood glucose (hypoglycemia) Renal function (creatinine, urea) Liver function tests (bilirubin, AST, ALT) Blood cultures  (if co-infection suspected

Slide 20
IMAGING / SPECIAL INVESTIGATIONS Chest X-ray : If respiratory distress or pulmonary edema is suspected Ultrasound abdomen : Rarely, for hepatosplenomegaly assessment or complications Lumbar puncture : Only if cerebral malaria is suspected and meningitis needs to be excluded Other tests  (for severe cases): Electrolytes (sodium, potassium) Coagulation profile if disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is suspected

Slide 21
MEDICAL MANAGEMENT  Assessment Before Treatment Confirm diagnosis using  microscopy or rapid diagnostic test (RDT) . Assess severity: uncomplicated vs severe malaria. Evaluate  age, weight, hydration status, comorbidities , and risk of complications.

Slide 22
MANAGEMENT OF UNCOMPLICATED MALARIA First-line treatment  (WHO-recommended Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy, ACT): Artemether-lumefantrine (AL) Artesunate-amodiaquine (ASAQ) Dosing based on weight  of the child Supportive care: Antipyretics for fever (e.g., paracetamol) Oral hydration Nutritional support

Slide 23
MANAGEMENT OF SEVERE MALARIA Hospitalization required . Intravenous antimalarial therapy : Intravenous  artesunate  (preferred) Alternatively, quinine if  artesunate  unavailable. Supportive care : Treat hypoglycemia (glucose infusion) Manage severe anemia (blood transfusion if indicated) Treat seizures (e.g., diazepam) Correct electrolyte imbalances Oxygen therapy for respiratory distress Monitor vital signs, urine output, and neurological status

Slide 25
NURSING CONSIDERATION  Monitor vital signs and level of consciousness regularly. Assess and record fever patterns. Administer antimalarial medications as prescribed, ensuring correct dosing. Maintain hydration and monitor fluid balance. Treat complications such as hypoglycemia, anemia, and seizures promptly. Provide supportive care (antipyretics, oxygen therapy if needed). Educate caregivers on recognizing warning signs of severe malaria. Encourage proper nutrition and rest. Monitor for adverse effects of medications. Document all observations and interventions accurately.

Slide 26
PUBLIC HEALTH CONSIDERATION  Promote the use of  insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs)  in households. Implement  indoor residual spraying (IRS)  in endemic areas. Conduct  community health education  on malaria prevention. Encourage early healthcare-seeking for febrile illness in children. Support malaria  screening and testing programs  in schools and communities. Advocate for vaccination where available (e.g., RTS,S malaria vaccine). Promote environmental control measures (removing stagnant water, clearing bushes). Monitor and report malaria cases for surveillance and control programs. Collaborate with local health authorities for outbreak response. Support policies for affordable and accessible malaria treatment and prevention.
