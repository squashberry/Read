Asthma is one of the most common chronic conditions affecting children worldwide. It is characterized by inflammation and narrowing of the airways, which leads to recurrent episodes of wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath

Slide 5
RISK FACTORS OF ASTHMA  Family History: Children with a family history of asthma or other allergic conditions (e.g., allergic rhinitis, eczema) have an increased risk. Allergen Exposure: Exposure to indoor allergens (e.g., dust mites, pet dander, mold) and outdoor allergens (e.g., pollen) can trigger asthma symptoms. Air Pollution: Both indoor (e.g., tobacco smoke, biomass fuel smoke) and outdoor pollutants have been linked to the development and exacerbation of asthma Viral Respiratory Infections: Early childhood respiratory infections, particularly those caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), can predispose children to asthma.

CLINICAL PRESENTATIOM Wheezing: A high-pitched whistling sound during breathing, especially during exhalation Coughing, often worse at night or early in the morning Shortness of Breath: Difficulty breathing, particularly during physical activity. Chest Tightness: A sensation of pressure or constriction in the chest. Cyanosis  Chest pain

Diagnosis History, physical examination, and the pattern of symptoms. Spirometry: In children old enough to perform lung function tests, spirometry helps assess the degree of airflow limitation and its reversibility. Allergy Testing: Identifying specific allergens can help tailor avoidance strategies.

ROLES OF NURSES  Assess respiratory status Monitor vital signs Check oxygen saturation Monitor peak expiratory flow rate Position patient upright (High Fowler’s) Administer oxygen therapy Administer prescribed bronchodilators (e.g., Salbutamol) Administer anticholinergics (e.g., Ipratropium bromide) Administer corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone) Maintain IV access (if severe)
