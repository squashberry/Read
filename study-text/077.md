Slide 4
Importance Milestones act as benchmarks; missing or significantly delaying them may signal underlying conditions such as autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, or hearing/vision problems. Early detection allows timely referrals for further evaluation and interventions. They provide a framework for pediatricians, family physicians, therapists, and other clinicians to assess whether a child is on track.

Slide 5
Cont. Recognizing delays helps in designing individualized care plans, including speech therapy, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or educational support. Early interventions have been shown to significantly improve long-term outcomes.

Slide 6
Cont. Clinicians use milestones to counsel parents on what to expect at different ages. This helps set realistic expectations, reduces unnecessary anxiety, and promotes positive parenting practices. Tracking developmental milestones at a population level helps in identifying risk factors (e.g., malnutrition, environmental toxins, poor access to healthcare).

Slide 7
Prenatal Stage (Conception to Birth) Germinal Stage (0–2 weeks): Fertilized egg (zygote) divides and implants in the uterine wall. Embryonic Stage (2–8 weeks): Major organs and body structures begin forming. Rapid cell differentiation.

Slide 8
Cont. Fetal Stage (9 weeks–birth): Growth and refinement of structures, development of movement, senses, and viability around 24 weeks.

Slide 9
Infancy (Birth to 2 years) Physical: Rapid growth in height and weight, brain development, motor skills (sitting, crawling, walking). Cognitive: Language acquisition begins, sensorimotor learning (object permanence). Social/Emotional: Attachment to caregivers, early emotional expressions.

Slide 10
Early Childhood   (2–6 years) Physical: Slower but steady growth, fine motor skills improve (drawing, dressing). Cognitive: Imaginative play, symbolic thinking, rapid vocabulary expansion. Social/Emotional: Development of self-concept, social play, early moral understanding.

Slide 11
Middle Childhood (6–12 years) Physical: Steady growth, improved strength and coordination. Cognitive: Logical thinking (concrete operations), mastery of basic academic skills. Social/Emotional: Peer relationships gain importance, increased independence, self-regulation.

Slide 12
Adolescence (12–18 years) Physical: Puberty, sexual maturation, rapid growth spurt. Cognitive: Abstract thinking (formal operations), identity exploration, future planning. Social/Emotional: Striving for independence, peer influence strong, identity vs. role confusion (Erikson).

Slide 13
Early Adulthood (18–40 years) Physical: Peak physical health and strength in 20s, gradual changes begin in late 30s. Cognitive: Advanced problem-solving, creativity, career focus. Social/Emotional: Intimacy vs. isolation (forming relationships, family, career building).

Slide 14
Middle Adulthood (40–65 years) Physical: Signs of aging (graying hair, wrinkles, reduced stamina), possible onset of chronic conditions. Cognitive: Stable intellectual abilities, expertise, and wisdom grow; some slowing of processing speed. Social/Emotional: Generativity vs. stagnation (focus on work, family, contributing to society).

Slide 15
Late Adulthood (65 years and beyond) Physical: Decline in strength, sensory abilities, immune function; increased risk of illness. Cognitive: Some experience memory decline, others maintain strong intellectual abilities; dementia risk increases. Social/Emotional: Integrity vs. despair (life review, acceptance of mortality, coping with losses).

Slide 16
Summary Prenatal to  Birth: Biological foundation Infancy to Adolescence: Growth, learning, identity formation Adulthood: Work, relationships, contribution Old Age: Reflection, wisdom, decline
